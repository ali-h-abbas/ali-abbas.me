Acorn theory is James Hillman's proposal, developed in _The Soul's Code: In Search of Character and Calling_, that each person is born with a defining image - a "soul code" that carries the character and calling of that life the way an acorn holds the blueprint of the oak. Against the dominant developmental story that we are products of genetics plus environment, Hillman argues for a third factor: an innate image that _selects_ experience as much as it is shaped by it, revealing itself in childhood obsessions, refusals, and seemingly irrational passions. The biography is read backwards - not "what made this person?" but "what was this person always aiming at?"

The theory deliberately revives Plato's [[Myth of Er]], in which each [[soul]] chooses its lot before birth and is assigned a [[daimon]] - a guiding spirit that accompanies the person and steers them toward their chosen pattern, even as the memory of the choice is lost. Hillman, founder of [[archetypal psychology]], translates the myth psychologically rather than metaphysically: the daimon shows up as the persistent tug of calling, the symptom that refuses to be cured because it protects the calling, the "problem" in childhood that turns out to be the gift announcing itself early. In this it extends [[Carl Jung]]'s notion of [[individuation]] - but where Jung emphasizes the _process_ of becoming whole, Hillman emphasizes the _image_ that was there from the start.

The acorn is not a fixed fate but a character to be grown into - it requires soil, water, and sunshine, and can be neglected, distorted, or refused. This reframes [[purpose]] away from goal-setting toward _listening_: attending to what keeps returning in one's private thoughts, what one keeps circling back to no matter what else occupies the day. It offers a mythic counterweight to the purely constructed view of [[identity]] - suggesting that [[narrative identity]] is not invented from nothing but discovers a pattern already latent, the way the oak is neither chosen by nor imposed upon the acorn.

Understanding acorn theory means holding it as psychological myth rather than empirical claim: a way of seeing lives that restores depth, necessity, and [[meaning]] to individual character - and a direct response to [[the meaning crisis]]'s flattening of persons into products of circumstance.
