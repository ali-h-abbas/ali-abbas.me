The Upper Paleolithic Transition (~40,000 BCE) marks the point at which _recognizably human_ culture emerges - not an absolute origin of meaning-making, which traces deep into our evolutionary heritage, but the threshold where the evidence becomes unmistakable. Representational and symbolic art appears: sculpture, cave painting, music. The first calendars emerge - symbolic tracking of moon phases and days, remarkably _before_ numeracy. [[John Vervaeke]] frames the shift as a **software change, not a hardware change**: the brain itself (~160,000 years old by then) wasn't anatomically changing; how it was being _used_ was - a claim he grounds in the work of David Lewis-Williams, Matt Rossano, and Michael Winkelman.

The context was existential pressure. A near-extinction bottleneck preceded the transition - population crunched to perhaps ~10,000 individuals between roughly 70,000 and 30,000 years ago, tied to climatic upheaval at the end of the last ice age and a supervolcanic eruption in Africa. The pressure drove migration to the coasts and, on Vervaeke's reading, a distinctive cognitive-cultural response: humans began solving problems _between_ minds rather than only within them. [[Distributed Cognition]] - networks of brains linked by language, [[symbols]], and practice - became the species' decisive adaptation, and [[Ritual]] emerged to extend trust beyond the kinship group, making cooperation with strangers possible.

This is also the horizon where [[Shamanism]] appears as humanity's first psycho-technology, where [[initiation]] practices begin as costly signals of commitment, and where the capacity for mindsight - reading others' mental states, and thereby one's own - opens the door to [[metacognition]] and [[mindfulness]]. In Gebser's terms it resonates with the emergence of [[magical consciousness]] out of the [[Archaic Consciousness|archaic]] structure: a new mode of [[participation]] in reality, dense with [[Mythology]] in embryo.

The transition matters for [[the meaning crisis]] because it locates meaning-making at the root of what makes us human: the same symbolic, participatory, communal capacities that emerged here are the ones modernity has eroded. The crisis, on this view, is not the loss of something optional but a disruption of the species' oldest operating system.
