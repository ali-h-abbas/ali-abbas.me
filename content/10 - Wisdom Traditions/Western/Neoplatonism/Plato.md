Plato (c. 428–348 BCE), student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, stands at the headwaters of Western [[philosophy]] - Whitehead's quip that the tradition is "a series of footnotes to Plato" understates how thoroughly his questions still frame ours. His central vision is of a [[reality]] behind appearance: the eternal [[form]]s, apprehended not by the senses but by trained intellect, with [[the Good]] as the source that illuminates all the rest. The allegory of the cave dramatizes the human condition this implies - prisoners mistaking shadows for the real, and [[philosophy]] as the turning of the [[soul]] toward the light.

Plato's method was as influential as his metaphysics. Writing in dialogues rather than treatises, he made [[dialectic]] - truth-seeking through structured conversation - the engine of insight, and preserved Socrates' practice of exposing what we do not know as the beginning of [[Wisdom]]. His account of knowledge as [[anamnesis]] (recollection), the soul's kinship with the [[intelligible realm]], and participation ([[methexis]]) as the relation binding particulars to their [[form]]s furnished the conceptual architecture that [[Neoplatonism]] would later systematize into the doctrine of [[Emanation & Descent]] from [[The One]].

The mythic Plato matters as much as the dialectical one. The [[Myth of Er]] gives the West its great image of the soul choosing its life and receiving its [[daimon]]; the _Symposium_ makes [[Eros]] the daimonic ladder from bodily beauty to [[Beauty]] itself; the [[Timaeus]] renders the cosmos as a living being crafted toward the good. Plato consistently reached for myth precisely where argument runs out - a recognition that some truths about [[soul]] and [[meaning]] travel only in story, and a bridge between rational philosophy and the [[mystery schools]] whose initiatory patterns his images echo.

His long afterlife runs through [[Neoplatonism]], [[Hermeticism]], [[Christian esotericism]], and Renaissance revival into modern depth psychology - [[Carl Jung]]'s [[Archetype|archetypes]] and Hillman's [[acorn theory]] are Platonic inheritances - while Aristotle's transformation of the Forms into [[Formal Causation]] carried his insight into the analysis of nature itself. To read Plato is to encounter the source code of the Western [[wisdom traditions]].
