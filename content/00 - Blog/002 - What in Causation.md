## What is Causation?

At its simplest, causation answers "Why is something the way it is? What explains it existence or behaviour?"

In Western thinking, we credit Aristotle for recognizing that this one question can be answered in radically different ways:

1. [[Material Causation]] - "Why is the sculpture the way it is?" _Because it is made of out of marble_
2. [[Efficient Causation]] - "Why did the vase break?" _Because I dropped it_ (past event pushing forward present effect)
3. [[Formal Causation]] - "Why is it a vase and not a bowl?" _Because of its [[form]]/pattern_
4. [[Final Causation]] - "Why does the acorn grow into an oak?" _Because that is its purpose/end_ (future goal that directs present development)

In the aforementioned video, Morgan explicates the issue with modern science is that it chose to privilege _only_ efficient and material cause. This has proven itself immensely valuable in matters of _prediction_ and _control_, but this deliberate narrowing misses the full story, and has huge implications with regards to how we interface with the world

## Why it matters

We might chalk much of this up to philisophers obsessing over semantics, but this understanding of causation forms the baselines behind how we think, view the world, and act.

### It Determines What You Think is Real

If we only look through efficient causes (mechanical push and pull) then

- purpose is an illusion (projected from us onto mindless matter)
- meaning is subjective (not in the world itself)
- consciousness is a biproduct and not causal
- Free will is impossible

If formal and final cause are incorporated then

- Pattern and [[meaning]] are objective features of reality
- Living things genuinely _aim_ at their ends
- Consciousness might be a genuine cause
- Purpose isn't imposed but inherent

### It Determines What Counts as an Explanation

Are you who you say you are because of some combination of efficient causes e.g. your genes, your biological makeup, your environment? Or is there something about what is formal or final that determines it - patterns, harmonies, structures and resonances that speak to you specifically.

The modern scientific worldview abandons the latter as unscientific, fanciful, or artistic - it relegates it to something "unreal" as it is not verifiable. Formscapes' argument is that this is a revelation of science's boundary - and that this boundary was well understood by pioneers of science such as Newton, who's private writings spend much more time examining formal and final causes than efficient ones

### It Determines How You Understand yourself

James Hillman's _Souls Code_ asks: Are you the product of your past (your trauma, your genes) or are you being pulled towards your destiny (via your calling, or [[daimon]])?

The answer? Probably a bit of both! And in all likelihood, we carry the beacon of formal and final cause with us in the art we enjoy - regardless of what science thinks.

### The Practical Question

Where this discussion gets particularly interesting to me is whether systems designed within a limited scientific worldview create predictable blind spots - and whether recognizing formal and final causation offers us a way to see them.

Consider human motivation: When we limit our understanding to statistical patterns and measurable metrics, we implicitly assume that _knowing the efficient causes_ (genes, environment, dopamine levels) is sufficient to explain behavior. But this misses the formal dimension - _what resonates_, the patterns and harmonies that speak to us specifically. It misses the final dimension - what calls us, the sense of purpose and direction that animates choice.

The consequences compound in systems we're now designing. When we architect automation rooted solely in efficient and material causes, we create systems with:

- **No concept of form**: Pattern recognition without understanding _meaning_
- **No concept of finality**: Optimization without purpose beyond their programmed goals
- **No way to ask**: "Is this aligned with what things are actually _for_?"

This isn't a reason to reject science or modern technology. Newton himself didn't. Instead, it's an invitation to ask: **What if we designed our systems - our technology, our organizations, our institutions - with awareness of all four causes? How could we?** What if efficiency wasn't the only measure? What if we built in recognition of formal patterns and human purpose? Do we truly understand _ourselves_ well enough to extrapolate those externally?

The philosophical point isn't academic. It's practical. In a world increasingly shaped by systems that _only see_ efficient causation, recovering formal and final cause becomes an act of reclamation of what gives our work meaning, what our creations are actually _for_.

And here's what I suspect: in the work we do designing systems and automating processes, we're often running on assumptions we've never examined. We assume efficiency is enough. We treat meaning and purpose as nice-to-haves, not load-bearing walls. But what if they're not? What if systems that ignore formal and final causation aren't just incomplete - they're actually fragile, unsustainable, and bound to collapse - because they've severed themselves from the very things that make humans _want_ to use them, work within them, and trust them?
